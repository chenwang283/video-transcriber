## Label key

**Error origin** — `Organic` (real habit) · `Lesson-Induced` (expected by lesson) · `Mixed` (lesson-induced with a real sub-problem inside)

**Evidence source** — `Student Side` (student said/did) · `Tutor Diagnostic` (tutor named the problem after seeing it) · `Tutor Setup` (tutor planned the stumble)

**Evidence type** — `Observable` (quoted/paraphrased from transcript) · `Inference` (read from context — must cite the observable fact behind it)

Quick term defines used in this report:
- **Command of Evidence:** SAT questions that ask the student to find the line of text that best supports or weakens a claim.
- **Hypothesis / claim / conclusion:** the researcher's main point in the passage.
- **Part-right-part-wrong:** an SAT rule. If any single piece of an answer choice is wrong, the whole answer choice is wrong, even if the rest sounds right.

## Strategic Issue 1: Reading without summarizing or predicting

### 1. Before — How the student was solving these questions

- Reads the whole passage start to finish.
- Tries to hold all of it in short-term memory.
- Goes straight to the answer choices.
- Picks the answer that sounds closest to what they remember.

### 2. Why that default doesn't work

The student picks answers that restate the passage instead of answers that actually do what the question asks.

**Example: LINE transposon question (Command of Evidence, textual)**
`Origin: Mixed`

- **Student Side** · `Observable`
  > *"to me the best one that matches is B, because... it says the transphonsin family is tied to the species capacity for, I guess, intelligence. And so humans are advanced, so B is saying that the human brain and the hippocampus, it contains multiple transposons from the LINE family. So that supports the hypothesis that, you know, intelligent."* `[00:26:17]`

  The student picked B. The correct answer was A.

- **Tutor Diagnostic** · `Observable`
  > *"answer choice B is actually like the second most picked answer choice... most of them actually pick B as well instead of A... it's literally just a summary of this top part."* `[00:32:06]`

  The tutor names the trap: B restates a sentence from the passage about humans. It does not support the hypothesis about other species.

- **Genuine sub-component**
  The student calls out the same trap on their own: *"I realize the answer I chose is kind of a summary of what you underlined at the top. But it's tricky because I think they assume you're not paying attention to that. Like I wasn't paying attention to it."* `[00:31:49]` This is a real habit, not just a stumble from a new strategy: reading for the gist of the passage, then picking what sounds like the passage.

### 3. What the tutor changed

1. Read the question first. Catch the keyword: support, weaken, or complete.
2. Read the passage in chunks of 1–2 sentences.
3. After each chunk, stop and put it in plain words.
4. Find the sentence with the claim, conclusion, or hypothesis.
5. Predict what the right answer should say before opening the answer choices.
6. Match the prediction to the choices. Cross off anything that doesn't match.

### 4. After — The student using the new process

**Example: 50,000-novels conclusion question (Command of Evidence, textual)**

- **Student Side** · `Observable`
  > *"I would say that, um, the researchers took a large sample from all over, and they used software to count references, whether specific time or implied time."* `[00:37:08]`

  The student paused after a passage chunk and put it in plain words — opposite of holding everything in memory.

- **Student Side** · `Observable`
  > *"I'm going to guess that has something to do with electricity and how that affects me. Maybe like staying up later because there's light."* `[00:38:28]`

  The student predicted the answer before reading the choices. The actual answer choice B matched the prediction directly.

- **Tutor Diagnostic** · `Observable`
  > *"You did a phenomenal job with that. Normally students struggle a lot in that, and like we end up spending like 3 minutes just sitting here just trying to figure it out. But you got there really quick."* `[00:38:45]`

  The tutor flags this as unusually fast for a hard-difficulty question.

**Example: Nucleobase graph question (Command of Evidence, graph)**

Adds new information: shows the prediction step on a graph question, which is a known weak spot for this student.

- **Tutor Diagnostic** · `Observable`
  > *"Wait, I'm gonna stop you real quick. Remember to make that prediction."* `[00:59:08]`

  The student went straight to the answer choices. The tutor stopped them mid-step.

- **Student Side** · `Observable`
  > *"I would predict the 2 top ones because it says it's not detected in the soil sample. So perfect, not contaminated."* `[01:00:22]`

  The student looked back at the graph, made a prediction based on the conclusion ("nucleobases formed in space, not from Earth contamination"), and then matched it to answer A correctly.

## Strategic Issue 2: Holding wrong answers instead of crossing them out

### 1. Before — How the student was solving these questions

- Reads each answer choice once.
- If anything sounds maybe-true, keeps it on the table as a "could be."
- Cycles back through the kept answers instead of cutting them.
- Picks by gut, not by evidence.

### 2. Why that default doesn't work

The student wastes time on dead answers and lands on the wrong one when two "could-bes" look similar.

**Example: Portrait miniature question (Main Idea)**
`Origin: Organic`

- **Student Side** · `Observable`
  > *"Um, um. I— oh my goodness, all these answers are so weird... I can't cross out all these answers. Okay, um... Wait, this one also kind of sounds right, but I think you have to, like, assume something about the text... I'm, like, so done. Okay. Maybe it is A."* `[01:19:44]`

  The student cycled through A, B, C, D, said "I don't know" or "I can't cross out" four times, then committed to A.

- **Tutor Diagnostic** · `Observable`
  > *"I noticed that you are like, you know, you're reading something, you're like, eh, it could be true, I'll hold onto that... it's just hard for you to eliminate answer choice when in your head, you know, you're not that confident in eliminating it."* `[01:37:48]`

  The tutor names the habit directly and adds it to the coaching list.

### 3. What the tutor changed

1. Break each answer choice at the commas. Most SAT answers have 2–4 parts.
2. After each part, ask: is this part true to the passage?
3. If even one part is wrong, the whole answer is wrong. Cross it off.
4. Stop reading the rest of that answer once one part fails.

### 4. After — The student using the new process

**Example: Ethiopian soil text structure question**

- **Student Side** · `Observable`
  > *"It introduces an unresolved scientific question. True. Presents the research team hypothesis pertaining to that question. True. And then describes an observation made by the team that conflicts with that hypothesis. True. So I don't know, I think that's the right answer, but I'll just give the other ones."* `[01:52:48]`

  The student broke choice B into three parts and checked each against the passage. Then ran the same check on C and D and cut them when one part failed.

- **Student Side** · `Observable`
  > *"That's why I was so confident that it was B 'cause all the other ones were like, oh."* `[01:54:34]`

  The student names the change directly. Compare to the cycling in the Before.

- **Tutor Diagnostic** · `Observable`
  > *"You did a— I'm not even joking, that was a phenomenal job breaking down each of those answer choices."* `[01:54:12]`

  The tutor flags this as a notably strong independent performance — the last question of the session.
